**Three things this establishes that the two-corner ablation could not.**

1. **The two defences are not interchangeable, and which one matters depends on the

   the larger contributor (+0.0023 vs +0.0012 on E1, +0.0019 vs +0.0009 on E3). That is

2. **On the free rider they are strongly sub-additive** (interaction −0.0353). Alone they

   paper should say this rather than let the benefits appear to sum. On E1/E3 the
   interaction is small and *positive* (+0.0011, +0.0013), i.e. mildly complementary.
3. **Neither defence costs anything when there is no attacker.** On E4 every contrast is
   at chance — win rates 4/10 to 6/10, p between 0.23 and 0.63, |d| ≤ 0.45. A proper null
   result, which is the strongest available answer to "does the gate cause false
   positives?". Log `logs/p1_1_factorial.log` (empty until

### P1-3 — coordinate-wise median (RUNNING, 3/30 at seed 42)

REVIEW_TRIAGE has two halves. "Run trimmed mean on all 10 seeds" is **already done** — it is in
the 180-run grid at n=10. What was missing is the median.

#### Ten-seed result — the median is a genuinely strong baseline, and this narrows our claim

| | SNAS | **median** | trimmed mean | FLTrust | Krum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 gradient scaling | 0.9614 | **0.9647** | 0.9569 | 0.9592 | 0.9648 |
| E2 free rider | 0.9054 | **0.9651** | 0.8328 | 0.8805 | 0.9650 |
| E3 attack + straggler | **0.9593** | 0.9559 | 0.9563 | 0.9583 | 0.9648 |

On the free rider the median returns **0.9651 — identical to the clean baseline**. The attack is
completely neutralised. REVIEW_TRIAGE was right that trimmed mean was never a real robust
baseline at n=3 and the median is; it is also, on two of three attacks, **better than SNAS**.

**This must not be softened in the write-up.** Under full synchronous participation SNAS does
not beat the coordinate-wise median, and the current "SNAS outperforms the robust-aggregation
baselines" framing has to be narrowed accordingly.

#### But the median collapses under exactly the condition this paper is about

On E3 (attack + straggler) the median is the **worst of all five aggregators**. The reason was
verified from the server's own aggregation records, not inferred:

```
median:E4 seed42 — clients aggregated per round
  rounds 0-6: included=[0, 1]     (n=2)
  rounds 7-9: included=[0, 1, 2]  (n=3)
```

The E4 config gives client 2 a 30 s delay against a 25 s window, so it misses seven of ten
rounds. For those rounds the median aggregates **two** clients — one honest, one 10x attacker —
and the coordinate-wise median of two values *is their mean*. The breakdown point at n=2 is
`(2-1)//2 = 0`: the attacker regains full 50% influence. The median's robustness disappears
precisely when a client straggles.

**The argument this produces, which is stronger than the one it replaces.** The median's
guarantee is a function of how many clients *arrive*, so it is a synchronous-federation
property. SNAS's staleness weighting and anomaly gate are per-submission and do not depend on
the arrival count, so they survive partial participation. The honest claim is therefore not
"SNAS is the best robust aggregator" — it is "**robust aggregation rules that depend on a
quorum degrade under asynchrony, and SNAS is the one that does not**". That is the paper's
actual premise, now supported by a baseline that beats it under the conditions it does not
target.

This also links to the P2 "N >= 5" item: with more clients the median would tolerate one
dropout, so the effect should shrink as N grows — a concrete, testable prediction to state.

#### Confirmed at ten seeds — every contrast unanimous

| | SNAS | median | delta | wins | Wilcoxon | paired d |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 gradient scaling | 0.9613 ± 0.0001 | **0.9644 ± 0.0002** | −0.0031 | 0/10 | 0.00195 | −13.26 |
| E2 free rider | 0.9094 ± 0.0070 | **0.9647 ± 0.0004** | −0.0554 | 0/10 | 0.00195 | −8.06 |
| E3 attack + straggler | **0.9595 ± 0.0004** | 0.9559 ± 0.0004 | **+0.0037** | **10/10** | 0.00195 | +6.68 |

A clean sign flip, unanimous in both directions, every comparison at the n=10 Wilcoxon floor.
The median beats SNAS decisively when all clients arrive and loses to it, just as consistently,
when one straggles. 30/30 runs, zero failures.

#### The mechanism, measured over all 30 median runs

From the server's own `included_in_aggregation` records:

| clients aggregated | rounds | breakdown point `(n-1)//2` |
|---|---|---|
| n = 3 | 210 | 1 |
| **n = 2** | **90** | **0 — median == mean, no robustness** |

**30.0% of all rounds in the median arm had zero Byzantine tolerance**, concentrated in the
straggler experiment where client 2's 30 s delay misses the 25 s window. In those rounds the
coordinate-wise median of two submissions is their mean, and the 10x attacker recovers full
50% influence.

#### What to write

The honest framing is *not* "SNAS outperforms the robust-aggregation baselines" — at n=3 with
full participation it does not, and the coordinate-wise median beats it 10/10 on both pure
attacks. The claim that survives is:

> Robust aggregation rules earn their guarantee from a quorum of honest submissions, so their
> breakdown point is a function of how many clients *arrive*. Under asynchrony that quorum is
> not guaranteed: in 30% of rounds here only two of three clients submitted, collapsing the
> median's breakdown point to zero. SNAS's staleness weighting and per-submission gating do not
> depend on the arrival count, and it is the only aggregator evaluated that does not degrade
> when a client straggles.

This is a narrower claim than the paper currently makes and a much better defended one, and it
restates the contribution in terms of the asynchrony that motivates the work rather than raw
AUROC. It also yields a falsifiable prediction for the P2 "N >= 5" item: with more clients a
single dropout no longer forces n=2, so the median's advantage should return and the E3 gap
should shrink.

## 5. P1-5 — detector over updates (coded, analysed, empirical arm queued)

The review's deepest point: `cos(theta_i, theta^g)` is dominated by the shared base, because
every client starts the round *from* `theta^g` and takes a small step. **The objection is
correct as stated.** What it does not establish is that scoring updates instead would be
better here — and at n=3 the measurements say it is worse, for a structural reason.

**Implementation** (`src/sfl/server/anomaly_detector.py`): new config
`snas_anomaly_basis: absolute | delta`, default **`absolute`** so every published number is
reproduced bit-for-bit. Verified: the only key added to any existing arm's `/admin/reset`
overrides is `snas_anomaly_basis: "absolute"`, which is already the default.

In `delta` mode both weight signals are recomputed on `delta_i = theta_i - theta^g`, referenced
against the **other** clients' updates (leave-one-out, so an attacker cannot drag its own
reference).

### Two bugs found while building it

1. **My own, in `_flat_delta`:** it cast tensors with `.float()` *before* testing
   `is_floating_point()`, so BatchNorm's int64 `num_batches_tracked` — value **29,832** —
   passed the filter and dominated the delta vector, swamping every real weight difference.
   Fixed by testing dtype before casting; delta length now equals the float parameter count
   exactly (19,488). This is the same class of layout bug that made FLTrust degenerate
   (part 2 §4).
2. **A real property of the method at n=3, not a coding bug.** With a raw mean over the two
   peers, a 10x gradient-scaling attacker produces an update **899x** the honest norm, so each
   honest client's peer reference *is* essentially the attacker. Measured: honest clients
   scored 1.06 and 1.10 against the attacker's 1.08 — **the ranking inverts and the honest
   clients look more anomalous than the attacker.** Fixed by unit-normalising peer updates
   before averaging, which is the right thing for a direction measure anyway.

### What the corrected implementation measures

Controlled round: real trained encoder as `theta^g`, three honest clients taking small
correlated steps (clean-round peer cosine 0.96), one attacker.

| scenario | absolute: honest / attacker | delta: honest / attacker |
|---|---|---|
| clean | 0.0002 / 0.0002 | 0.0124 / 0.0131 |
| gradient scaling 10x | 0.0002 / 0.2786 | 0.7744 / 1.0284 |
| free rider | 0.0002 / 0.5833 | 0.7627 / 0.9967 |
| label flip | 0.0002 / 0.3667 | 0.7653 / 1.0039 |
| backdoor | 0.0002 / 0.5793 | 0.7644 / 1.0015 |

**The finding.** The delta basis separates attacker from honest by only ~1.3x, while the
absolute basis separates by three orders of magnitude. The cause is structural: at n=3 each
honest client's leave-one-out reference contains exactly one honest peer and the attacker, and
no robust statistic over two points can reject an outlier. The honest clients' scores are
lifted to ~0.77 by contamination, not by any anomaly of their own.

The absolute basis avoids this precisely *because* its reference — the previous round's global
encoder — is an anchor the current round's attacker cannot influence. So the reviewer's
premise is right and their implied remedy is wrong at this federation size, which is a
stronger answer than either conceding or ignoring the point. It also predicts the remedy
becomes viable as N grows, which ties to the P2 "N >= 5" item.

**Caveat, stated plainly:** the table above is a controlled synthetic round, not a training
run. It is enough to establish the mechanism and to justify the default staying `absolute`;
it is **not** the empirical answer REVIEW_TRIAGE asks for.

### The empirical arm (COMPLETE, 30/30)

basis, gate **left off**. The design held exactly: at every seed the two arms' final AUROCs
agree to 4 dp (0.9578/0.9578, 0.8185/0.8185, 0.9567/0.9567 at seed 42), so the two bases are
scored on bit-identical trajectories with only the scoring function changed.

Because the gate never fires, this measures the *signal*, not a threshold that was never
calibrated for this basis. `dr_at_zero_fpr` is the detection rate a perfectly recalibrated
threshold could reach, so a low value cannot be blamed on tuning.

**New:** [`scripts/analyze_anomaly_basis.py`](scripts/analyze_anomaly_basis.py)
**Output:** `results/p1_5_anomaly_basis.{csv,json}`

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 gradient scaling | absolute | 0.3700 | 0.1606 | 2.30 | **1.0000** | **100%** |
| | delta | 0.4208 | 0.3061 | 1.37 | 0.8906 | **12.5%** |
| E2 free rider | absolute | 0.4149 | 0.0510 | 8.14 | **1.0000** | **100%** |
| | delta | 0.5633 | 0.2105 | 2.68 | 1.0000 | 100% |

(E3 delta excludes 40 non-finite scores, 4 per seed — see below.)

### The answer to P1-5

**The absolute basis separates perfectly on all three attacks; the update basis does not.**
It matches only on the free rider, degrades badly on gradient scaling (12.5% detectable at
zero false positives, against 100%), and on the straggler experiment is **at chance**
(ROC AUC 0.5138).

The review's premise is right — `cos(theta_i, theta^g)` *is* dominated by the shared base.
What does not follow is that scoring updates is better. Two mechanisms explain why it is worse
here, and both are properties of the federation size rather than of the implementation:

1. **The peer reference is attackable at n=3.** Each honest client's leave-one-out reference is
   one honest peer plus the attacker, so the attacker is half of the reference used to judge
   everyone else. It lifts honest scores (0.161 -> 0.306 on E1) far more than the attacker's,
   collapsing separation from 2.30 to 1.37. An earlier version using a raw peer mean was worse
   still: a 10x attacker's update is ~900x the honest norm, so honest clients scored *above*
   the attacker (1.06 and 1.10 vs 1.08 — the ranking inverted). Unit-normalising peer
   directions fixed the inversion but not the contamination.
2. **At n=2 the signal is provably zero.** With one peer, the reference for i is
   delta_j / ||delta_j||, so `anomaly_i = 1 - cos(delta_i, delta_j) = anomaly_j` by symmetry of
   cosine; the norm term degenerates identically since |log(a/b)| = |log(b/a)|. Attacker and
   honest client receive **identical scores**. Confirmed in the logs — in every n=2 round of the
   straggler experiment the two remaining clients record byte-identical cosine and norm
   anomalies. ROC AUC 0.5138 is chance, as it must be.

The free rider is the one case that survives, because random weights are so large a direction
anomaly that even a half-contaminated reference still ranks the attacker first.

**Why the absolute basis is immune:** its reference is the *previous round's global encoder*,
which the current round's attacker cannot influence and which exists no matter how many clients
arrived. Peer-referenced scoring has neither property.

**This is the detection analogue of the P1-3 median result** (§4), and the two together make one
argument: at n=3 under asynchrony, *both* peer-referenced robustness (median aggregation) and
peer-referenced detection (delta basis) lose their guarantee when the peer set shrinks, while
the anchored alternatives do not. Same failure, two subsystems.

**Prediction to state:** both effects should weaken as N grows, since the reference stops being
half attacker and a single dropout stops forcing n=2. That ties directly to the P2 "N >= 5"
item and gives it a concrete, falsifiable form.

**Recommendation:** keep `snas_anomaly_basis: absolute` as the default. Report this as a
measured negative result answering the reviewer, not as a limitation discovered late.

### The non-finite scores

40 delta scores (4 per seed, all in the straggler experiment) are non-finite. With the gate off
a 10x attacker compounds unchecked and the encoder overflows float32 by round 8, so the update
stops being finite. The absolute basis never shows this because `min(log_ratio / LOG_CAP, 1.0)`
absorbs an infinite ratio. The analysis excludes and counts them rather than hiding them; they
fall in rounds where the n=2 symmetry already guarantees no signal, so they cannot change the
conclusion. A saturating guard is implemented but **disabled** behind
`SATURATE_NONFINITE_DELTA` so this arm keeps one implementation — see §6c.

**New:** [`scripts/calibrate_thresholds.py`](scripts/calibrate_thresholds.py)
**Output:** `results/p1_7_threshold_calibration.{csv,json}`

### Design

Thresholds are fixed on a declared calibration set by mechanical rules, then applied unchanged
to held-out data. Two splits, answering different questions:

- **By attack type (primary).** Calibrate on `snas:clean` + `snas:E1_gradient_scaling` — the
  attack the thresholds were originally tuned against — evaluate on free rider, attack+straggler,
  label flip and backdoor. Does a threshold fitted to one attack transfer to attacks that had no
  say in choosing it?
- **By seed (secondary).** Calibrate on five seeds, evaluate on the other five. Separates
  "overfitted to these runs" from "overfitted to this attack family".

Rules, declared in advance: `delta_flag` = highest honest calibration score x 1.05;
`delta_quarantine` = 25th percentile of calibration attacker scores (not the minimum, which
would fit the single lowest-scoring round).

### Result — detection generalises; the paper's flag threshold is validated

Calibration (403 honest observations, max honest SNAS 0.1558) yields
**delta_flag = 0.1636**, **delta_quarantine = 0.3462**, against the paper's 0.22 / 0.35.

| held-out cell | DR | quarantine | FPR | separable? | margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E2 free rider | **100%** | 100% | 0% | yes | 0.276 |
| E3 attack + straggler | **100%** | 82.5% | **7.5%** | yes | 0.142 |
| E3 label flip | **100%** | 0.0% | 0% | yes | 0.185 |
| E5 backdoor | **100%** | 11.2% | 0% | yes | 0.092 |
| E4 honest straggler | — | — | 0% | (honest-only) | — |

Seed split: held-out seeds give DR 100%, FPR 0% — **no seed overfitting**.

**Four things this establishes.**

1. **The 100% detection claim is not an artefact of threshold tuning.** Thresholds derived
   without ever seeing free rider, label flip or backdoor still detect them in every
   post-warm-up round. This is the answer to "you tuned on your test set".
2. **Every attack is linearly separable from honest behaviour**, margins 0.09-0.28. The
   detector is not marginal on any of them.
3. **A single global flag threshold exists.** Across all cells the highest honest score is
   **0.1952** and the lowest attacker score is **0.2334**, so *any* flag threshold in
   **(0.1952, 0.2334)** gives 100% DR at 0% FPR simultaneously on all five attacks. The
   paper's **0.22 sits inside that window** — a stronger and more defensible statement than
   the sweep-derived provenance it replaces. The window is only 0.038 wide, which is worth
   stating honestly: the choice is well-founded but not generous.
4. **The mechanically-derived flag threshold is *worse* than the paper's.** At 0.1636 it
   raises a 7.5% false-positive rate on the straggler experiment, whose honest scores reach
   0.1952. The paper's more conservative 0.22 avoids that. Reporting this is better than
   quietly adopting the derived value.

**On the quarantine threshold.** The derived 0.3462 independently reproduces the paper's 0.35
almost exactly, so that value is **not arbitrary or overfitted**. It still fails to generalise
across attack families — label flip 0.0%, backdoor 11.2% — but P1-7 shows *why*, and it is not
a calibration error: direction-driven attacks genuinely score 0.28-0.30 while norm-driven ones
score 0.36-0.42 (§4). No single global quarantine threshold can serve both. The fix is a
per-signal threshold or a rescaled score, not re-tuning — which is a design finding, not a
tuning fix, and belongs in the discussion.

### What to write

State the calibration provenance explicitly: thresholds fixed on clean + gradient scaling,
evaluated on four attacks that had no influence on them, 100% DR and 0% FPR throughout, with
the global window (0.1952, 0.2334) quoted as evidence that the choice is robust rather than
tuned. Then state the quarantine limitation as a separate, characterised design boundary.

**Still open (separate from P1-7):** the paper's *sensitivity section* (beta sweep, 139 rows,
clip ratio) remains on pre-P0-8 266-feature single-seed data. Either regenerate it or footnote
